Watching Metropolis

posted: 27.05.2026 ▶

I know I might be a little late - well 99 years too late to be exactly- but there is something about this movie that makes it so memorizable and special. The camera handling, the actors, the myth about the whole. When I read that Metropolis is coming back to my local cinema I was quite thrilled. I knew that this milestone of silent film is a must watch. Imagine getting offered 800.000 € for making a movie, hassling it up to double the budget and ending up spending 5 million and counting anyway. That’s exactly what regisseur Fritz Lang did. Though I didn’t convert the amount from Reichsmark into Euro you can get a picture of what his productions company thought of Lang afterwards. But the effort and investition formed into a great piece of art. It wasn’t a box office hit back then but influenced so many movies. Known as one of the first sci-fi movies, Metropolis has a more cultural inheritance.

Lang and his wife Thea von Harbou awoke different ideas that had support in following productions. The idealisation of flying vehicles is first brought on screen by Metropolis in the stock motion scenes of the city. The realistic perspective is partly broken by the turn of a plane around a corner that is absurdly unnatural. Here lay the limits for such an early production. The camera motion is quite modern. There are close ups, various effects and in one scene the camera swings forth and back like a kid on a playground to simulate a collapsing surrounding.

Especially interesting is a scene where part of the heart machine is shown with workers maintaining it at all time. The perspective is picked so that it reminds of a Sphinx. And so the perception changes when a bystander watches it too long: it transforms to Moloch. A kind of workers demon gobbling down its fuel: the workers themself. Lang used a by that time brand new technique called Schüfftan process where mirrors come to effort to optically simulate extra getting eaten by Moloch on record but in reality they jump into safety because there’s a hole in the mirror.

The story is relatively simple compared to the techniques used. Head of the city Joh Fredersen has a son and had a wife called Hel. She died while giving birth. She had another lover: Rotwang who is a crazy but successful scientist. Other than the father his son Freder is more open to the working class. Once he gets visited by poor Maria with a lot of children around her he can’t get her out of his head and searches for her. Rotwang hijacks Maria to put her face onto his human-machine. Everything is hold together through a motto: “Between brain and hands there must be a mediator: the heart.” This resembles the weimarian wish for harmony in a hard financial time. There are more allusions to the current situation in post war Germany. The human-machine celebrates big, unnecessary and meaningless fests with the upper 100 people of power. Just like many people in Berlin at that time. The event that a lower class is easily mobilized in an industrialized world is part of the truth that bloomed just a few years ahead. The nazis really liked the movie which is why Lang later thought not good about it. They cut it down to shorter playtime and less communist meaning and used it partly for propaganda.

I haven’t found any text of Tucholsky regarding Metropolis, who is a great critic from the weimarian republic who often discussed movies in the paper “Weltbühne”. He has always thought movies were nothing but silly and unworthy as art and good pastime. Though I’m sure he would have written different about Metropolis.

In the Dark

posted: 08.02.2026 ▶

Although not all analogue photowork requires absolute darkness it coined the term "darkroom" for doing prints, developing film and editing them. What exactly is refered to a darkroom depends individually: some use lightsealed bags where one just sticks their hands in and so does not witness any darkness, others make up whole rooms for the chemical processes of analog photography and to pretend being a bat. It comes handy to share such room with other photographs to divide purchases and continious costs. You can find this approach often in photography clubs. In my case it was more convenient to put up a darkroom hub in my kitchen:

Most time my kitchen simply is a kitchen. But when there's an exposed film or I want to do some prints, I convert my kitchen workplace and get on. This procedure is practical, spontanuous and not dependent on any time schedule other than mine. The only thing bothering me is that half a squaremeter is permanently occupied by the projector. It would be much more time consuming though to build my setup down every time. Furthermore I dont have that much manpower around normally. For the initial setup I had to ask a friend to help. You get an idea: with an unshared darkroom comes quite some laziness. But exactly that feeling of "it will fit" can come of use in all works in the dark. But to that I'll come in a later blog entry.

When setting up a darkroom for the first time need really not that much. Well quite. A seat, a workplace and awful expansive gadgets can be helpful but aren't necesary. For now a small room without windows (or good curtains) is alright when you get rid of any light srources. Close the door and boom: darkroom.
